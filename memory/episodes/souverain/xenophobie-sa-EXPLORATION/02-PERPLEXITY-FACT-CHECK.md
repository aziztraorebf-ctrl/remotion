## Historical Context of Xenophobic Violence in South Africa (1994-2024)

### The Emergence of Xenophobic Violence in Post-Apartheid South Africa

The transition to democracy in South Africa following the 1994 elections paradoxically coincided with the emergence of organized xenophobic violence—a phenomenon that initially surprised international observers given the anti-apartheid struggle's overtly internationalist ideology and the country's constitutional protections for non-citizens. The initial waves of xenophobic violence emerged in the mid-1990s in informal settlements and townships, targeting particularly visible immigrant populations including Mozambicans, Zimbabweans, and other African nationals who had begun arriving in South Africa seeking economic opportunities or fleeing regional conflicts. These early incidents were often framed by perpetrators and local politicians as responses to economic competition for informal sector jobs, housing, and services in contexts of severe structural unemployment and resource scarcity. The violence was not initially organized into coherent movements but rather emerged as sporadic mob actions, often tacitly supported or enabled by local authorities and sometimes framed within xenophobic political rhetoric that distinguished between "African foreigners" and South African citizens.

The decade from 1994 to 2005 witnessed recurring patterns of violence characterized by attacks on informal traders, particularly those from West African nations, arson against foreign-owned shops and homes, and orchestrated campaigns of forced expulsion from particular neighborhoods. These incidents, while documented by human rights organizations and international observers, did not command sustained international attention and were often treated as routine crime rather than systematic violence based on national origin. The scale of these incidents appears to have been substantial, with various documentation efforts by organizations like the Xenophobia Violence Monitor and reports from the Human Rights Commission of South Africa tracking hundreds of incidents annually during certain periods. The rhetoric accompanying these attacks often invoked competition for scarce resources, accusations of foreign involvement in drug trafficking or other crimes, and nationalist sentiments about "South African jobs for South Africans."

### The 2008 Xenophobic Violence Wave: A Watershed Moment

The xenophobic violence of May 2008 represented a qualitative escalation in scale, organization, and international visibility compared to earlier incidents. Over 600 foreign nationals were displaced internally, with many seeking refuge in temporary shelters or returning to neighboring countries. The 2008 violence was notable for its mob-based character, with attacks often carried out by groups of South African township residents against immigrant-owned businesses and homes. International media coverage was substantial, with the violence generating sustained attention from international human rights organizations and leading to a significant diplomatic response, including statements from the African Union and neighboring states.

The 2008 violence prompted significant institutional responses within South Africa, including the establishment of the Langa Commission of Inquiry and increased documentation efforts by civil society organizations. These institutional investigations provided more systematic recording of incidents, patterns, and causative factors than had previously existed. The period following 2008 saw increased international engagement on xenophobia in South Africa, with UN bodies, the African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights, and international NGOs producing reports attempting to document and analyze the phenomenon. However, the 2008 violence also served a concerning function in South African political discourse: it became a reference point that both normalized xenophobic sentiment and provided a baseline against which subsequent incidents were measured, potentially creating a framework where smaller-scale violence became routine rather than exceptional.

## Institutional Frameworks and Legal Protections

### The African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights

The African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights, adopted in 1981 and entered into force in 1986, establishes fundamental protections against discrimination and violence that would apply to African nationals subject to xenophobic attacks in any African country, including South Africa. Specific relevant articles include Article 2 (non-discrimination on any grounds including national origin), Article 3 (equal protection of the law), Article 4 (right to life and integrity), Article 5 (prohibition of torture and cruel treatment), and Article 15 (right to work under equitable and satisfactory conditions). The Charter establishes that these rights apply to all persons within the jurisdiction of state parties regardless of citizenship status, though states retain some capacity to implement different regulations for non-citizens in specified domains.

South Africa ratified the African Charter in 1996, making it legally bound by these obligations at the international level, though domestic implementation has proceeded unevenly. The African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights, established as the Charter's monitoring body, has issued communications and decisions addressing xenophobic violence in South Africa, though enforcement mechanisms remain limited beyond moral persuasion and reputational pressure. The Commission has documented complaints regarding xenophobic violence and has called on South Africa to strengthen protections, but the difference between legal obligation and actual protection on the ground remains substantial.

### South African Constitutional Protections

The South African Constitution, adopted in 1996 and representing one of the world's most progressive constitutional documents, includes significant protections against discrimination and violence that apply regardless of citizenship status.
